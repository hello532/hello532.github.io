Vetting a video editor for paid ads

Editor, DTC Outsourcing Playbook

Updated 10 September 2026 · 7 min

Vetting a video editor for paid ads starts with one distinction: a beautiful edit and a working ad are different products. Narrative editors build tension and pay it off at the end; ad editors put the payoff in the first two seconds because that is when the viewer decides. Hiring the first kind for the second job is how a launch ends up with a film nobody watches to the end.

Key takeaways

  • Write the hook yourself. Do not outsource the claim.
  • Watch samples muted. If nothing legible happens in the first two seconds, they edit for cinema, not feeds.
  • On delivery: watch it muted on a phone, check safe areas, verify claims, and confirm music rights.

Write the hook yourself

Do not outsource the claim. You know what the product does, who is annoyed by the alternative, and which objection kills the sale. Write one sentence a stranger would stop for, then hand the edit to someone who can execute it. An editor asked to invent positioning will default to product beauty shots with music, and you will have paid to learn nothing.

Vetting: what to look for in a portfolio

Signals to look for in an ad editor's reel, and why each one matters
SignalWhy it matters
Vertical-first workAds run in feeds. An editor whose reel is all 16:9 will crop badly and lose the subject.
Hook in the first two secondsWatch their samples with sound off. If nothing legible happens immediately, they edit for cinema, not feeds.
On-screen text and captionsAssume the sound is off. Burned-in captions and readable text overlays are table stakes.
Cuts driven by informationGood ad pacing changes shot when a new fact arrives, not on the beat of a music track.
Variation workAsk whether they have produced multiple hook variants of one edit. Testing needs variants, not one perfect film.
Handles supplied footageSome editors only work with material they shot. Confirm they will work with phone footage, stock, and product stills.
Ask one question before ordering: "Here is my hook line and my footage — what would you cut in the first two seconds?" A useful answer names a specific frame. A vague answer about "dynamic energy" tells you the rest of the job in advance.

Check on delivery

  • Watch it muted on a phone. If the offer is not clear without sound, it is not finished.
  • Check the safe areas. Platform interface elements overlay the top and bottom of vertical video. Text pushed to the edges gets covered by the caption box or buttons.
  • Verify the claims. Every on-screen word is your legal exposure, not the editor's. Read each frame of text as if a regulator were reading it.
  • Confirm music rights. Ask for the licence source in writing. Ad accounts get muted or flagged over audio, and "it came with the template" is not a licence.
  • Get the alternates. One video is not a test. Confirm the alternate hooks arrived and that only the opening differs, so a test tells you something.
  • Ask for the project file if you plan to iterate. Re-cutting from scratch each round is the slowest way to test.

What pushes the price up

Length, number of aspect ratios and hook variants, motion graphics, whether footage needs sourcing rather than editing, captioning, and rush turnaround. Scope by variants rather than by minutes — for paid ads, three usable 15-second variants beat one polished 60-second film.

FAQ

How do I tell a paid-ads editor from a narrative editor?

Watch their samples with sound off. If nothing legible happens in the first two seconds, they edit for cinema, not feeds. Ad editors put the payoff in the first two seconds because that is when the viewer decides.

Should I outsource the hook line?

No. Write the hook yourself. An editor asked to invent positioning will default to product beauty shots with music, and you will have paid to learn nothing.

What must a first ad cut contain?

The hook in the first two seconds, burned-in captions, a named aspect ratio and length, a call to action whose wording you supplied, and alternate hooks if you plan to test.

What should I check on delivery of an ad cut?

Watch it muted on a phone, check the safe areas, verify every on-screen claim, confirm music rights in writing, and get the alternate hooks so a test tells you something.
