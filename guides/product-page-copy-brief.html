Product page copy brief

Updated 4 September 2026 · reading time about 8 minutes

No copywriter can tell you why your product is worth buying. That answer sits in your spec sheet, your inbox, and the three objections you keep hearing in the buyer's own words. Send none of it and you get adjectives back — accurate, grammatical, and worth nothing on a product page.

The decision rule

Outsource when the raw material already exists: a spec sheet, the three objections you hear most often written out verbatim, and any real messages from customers. Do it yourself when none of that exists yet.

The do-it-yourself case is not a consolation prize, and it is not about saving anything. Writing the first draft yourself is how you discover that you cannot say what the product replaces, or that the objection you assumed was about money is really about fit. Your draft can be graceless. A writer can fix graceless. Nobody can fix empty. Hand over nothing and you pay to receive your own vagueness back in better grammar.

There is a second reason to write badly first. After twenty of your own sentences you can read a writer's samples properly, because you finally know what the page has to do. Before that, every sample looks fine. Choosing well is a separate skill: reading listings and vetting a seller.

Keep the order straight, too. The page comes before the ad that points at it, which is why it sits early in the 7-task launch outsourcing checklist. Blog articles, ad scripts and email sequences are separate orders with separate briefs — flows especially, which have their own structure and timings in the email flow brief. Keyword research sold as a service is also not copy. If you want the page to rank, treat that as a later conversation, and never let it reshape a sentence that was busy answering an objection.

Raw material is the ceiling on the deliverable

Nothing in the brief can raise a page above the quality of what went into it. Gather these before you order, and gather them in the words they were originally said in.

  • Specs that change a purchase decision. Not all of them. The ones someone would abandon an order over: dimensions, materials, compatibility, care, what is in the box, how long it lasts, what it does not do. A spec that changes nothing belongs in a table lower down the page, not at the top of a brief.
  • What the product replaces. Buyers arrive holding something already, even if that something is a compromise or a piece of tape. Copy that never names the alternative gives the reader nothing to compare against and no reason to move.
  • Objections, verbatim. Paste the sentences as they were written to you, typing errors and all. A paraphrased objection has already been softened by the person doing the paraphrasing, and the softened version is the one the writer will answer.
  • Praise, verbatim. The sentence buyers keep repeating is usually the differentiator you would never have picked yourself. Handed that line, a writer builds the page around it; denied it, they build the page around whatever you happened to mention first.
  • Return and refund reasons, if you have orders. Every return reason is an objection the page failed to answer before the sale. They belong on the page, pre-empted, even when they read as criticism.
  • Two competitor pages you rate, with the reason stated. "This one is good" is unusable. "This one puts the sizing chart above the description, and sizing questions are the mail I answer most" tells a writer precisely what to borrow — a structural decision, not a turn of phrase.

If you have no orders yet, say so in the brief rather than leaving the section blank. Substitute the questions asked by people who genuinely might buy: the ones in your messages, at a market stall, in a community you belong to. A writer who knows the well is dry will ask fewer questions of it. A writer facing an unexplained gap fills it.

What a usable deliverable contains

A title that names the product and its one differentiator. Not a slogan. A reader arriving from an ad or a search result needs to confirm in one glance that they are in the right place, and a clever title makes them confirm it twice.

An early block that answers the objection actually stopping the purchase. Writers file objections in an FAQ because it is tidy. Tidy is not the goal — a reader who scrolls past an unanswered doubt does not scroll back up for it later. Name the objection in the brief and name the block it must be answered in.

Scannable specifics where an adjective would be easier. "Premium quality" survives every draft because it costs nothing to write. The line that earns its place says what the material is, what it fits, what happens after a hundred washes, and what is in the box. Specifics also pre-empt the messages you would otherwise answer by hand for the next year.

An FAQ built only from questions real people asked. Invented questions are filler dressed as service, and they are easy to spot on delivery: they are the ones no customer would phrase that way. If you want a page title and meta description written as well, ask for them as separate labelled lines, and check the current length guidance where they will actually be displayed rather than trusting a number from an article.

Register, because "our brand voice" is not an instruction

"Write in our brand voice" only means something if the voice is documented somewhere a stranger can read it. When it is not, the phrase translates to "guess, and I will tell you when it is wrong", which is a revision round you funded to discover something you could have written in one line.

The fix is to choose one register and forbid mixing. Plain: short sentences, no metaphor, the product explained as you would explain it to a neighbour. Technical: specifications lead, the reader is assumed to know the vocabulary, claims are precise and narrow. Playful: personality carries the page, and the specifics still have to be there underneath. Any of the three can sell. A page that switches between them mid-block sells nothing, because the reader stops trusting the narrator.

Then paste two or three sentences of your own writing as the sample to match — an email you sent a customer, or the answer you gave when a friend asked what you sell. Not a brand you admire; your own words. A writer can match a sample. Nobody can match an adjective. Add a short list of words and phrases banned on this page while you are there, because the banned list is usually more revealing than the sample.

Fit the copy to the theme, not to a blank page

Copy is written into a layout, not onto an empty document. If the brief never names the blocks, the deliverable arrives as an essay, and then someone decides which half of it to delete — usually you, late, in a hurry. That deletion is where the proof and the objection answer disappear, because they sit at the end of each block where the cutting starts.

So ask for section headers that match your theme's own block names, a word budget per block, and an explicit instruction about which block carries the objection answer. Get the block names the boring way: open the theme editor, write down what is there in order, and paste that list into the brief. Fifteen minutes of that removes the entire category of copy that does not fit.

If the layout itself is still being built, brief the build and the copy against the same list of sections in the same order, using the landing page and theme build brief. Two people working from one section list produce a page that assembles. Two people working from separate instincts produce a page that has to be reconciled.

The boundary that causes disputes

Two different deliverables share one name. "Product page copy" can mean a document you paste in yourself, or the copy live on your store when the order closes. Both are legitimate. They are not the same job, they are not the same scope, and the difference has to be settled in writing before you order rather than negotiated once the draft exists.

Copy pasted into a live store means store access. If you choose that version, treat it like any build job: a staff account with the narrowest permissions that lets the work happen, work on a duplicated theme rather than the live one wherever possible, access removed the moment the order closes, and never your account password. An argument about who was supposed to paste is irritating. An argument that starts after someone held admin access to your live store is a different order of problem.

What you do not delegate

Four things stay with you regardless of who writes the sentences: the price, the offer, the guarantee, and every factual claim about the product. They go into the brief as decided facts, not as open questions. A writer needs to know the price to pitch against it; a writer must not be the person who chooses it.

A writer may well propose a bundle, a shipping promise, or a returns window that reads beautifully. Treat that as a suggestion, never as a deliverable. Those are commitments you have to fund and honour, and the page is not the place they get decided.

Claims are the sharper end of this. Facing a gap, a competent writer reaches for the plausible thing: aircraft-grade aluminium, hand-finished in Italy, certified organic, lasts a decade. None of it is malicious. All of it becomes yours the moment it is published, because the store name on the page is yours and the card statement says yours too. The writer moves on to the next order; you keep the sentence.

The mechanism that prevents this is simple and worth insisting on: ask the writer to mark every factual claim with the line of the brief it came from. Anything unmarked gets deleted rather than debated. Requiring it in the brief also filters the sellers who intended to pad the page with material they would have had to invent.

Invented claims about materials, origin, certification, or results are the fastest route to a chargeback, a pulled advertising account, or a takedown notice. They also arrive late, months after the page went live and long enough for the sentence to have been quoted back at you by a buyer or a regulator. Delete any claim you cannot trace to something you supplied, including the ones that read well. Especially those.

Ordering it

What moves a quote in this category is the word count and the number of pages, whether research is included — reading your reviews, talking to a customer, studying the competitor pages — or whether you are supplying everything, the number of revision rounds and what counts as one, and turnaround. A finished brief is the fastest route to quotes you can actually compare, because it removes the guesswork a seller would otherwise have to price for. Check on delivery

  • Every claim traces to something you supplied. Pick three sentences at random and ask which line of the brief each came from. An unanswerable one is a claim you now own.
  • The objection is answered above the fold on a phone, not filed in the FAQ.
  • Specifics appear where an adjective would have been easier — materials, measurements, compatibility, what is included.
  • Section headers match your theme's block names exactly, so pasting is mechanical rather than interpretive.
  • Word counts sit inside the budget per block, counted rather than described as roughly right.
  • No certification, origin, test result, or performance claim you did not supply appears anywhere, including in the FAQ and the meta description.
  • FAQ questions are ones real people asked. Invented questions read as service and deliver none.
  • The register holds to the last line. Drift shows up at the end, where attention ran out.
  • Nothing commits you to a price, an offer, a guarantee, or a delivery promise you have not agreed.

Where to look: website and product copywriting listings. If the images the copy sits beside are not sorted yet, start with the product photo brief template, because the main image and the first line of copy have to be making the same promise.

A product page is not a writing problem. It is a knowing problem, and writing is only the part where what you know becomes sentences. Collect the objections, the specs, and the words customers actually used, and a competent writer has everything they need. Skip that collection and the best writer you can find will hand you a page of adjectives, on time, exactly as ordered.
