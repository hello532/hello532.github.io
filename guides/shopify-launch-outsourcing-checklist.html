The 7-task launch outsourcing checklist

Updated 4 September 2026 · reading time about 9 minutes

A store does not fail because the founder drew a mediocre logo. It fails because the product page does not answer an objection, the first ad creative is unwatchable, and checkout breaks on mobile. This checklist covers the seven tasks that actually change those outcomes, in the order they block a launch.

Use the decision rule first. Every task below starts with a rule for keeping it in-house. Outsourcing something you have not yet decided is expensive: you pay a freelancer to guess at a decision only you can make. Two decisions then recur on every task here: whether the job wants a fixed price or an hourly contract, which you settle before writing the brief, and reading a listing and vetting a seller, which is the same skill in every category.

1. Logo and a minimum brand kit

Outsource when you already know the name, the market position, and roughly who the buyer is. Do it yourself when the name is still moving — a logo built on a name you abandon next week is money burned twice.

What you are buying is not "a nice mark". It is a set of files that survives the next two years: a primary lockup, a stacked or square variant for social avatars, a monochrome version for packaging and invoices, and defined colours and typefaces so every later asset matches.

Brief

Name, exact spelling and capitalisation:
One sentence on what the product is and who buys it:
Three brands whose visual tone I want to sit near (with links):
Two brands I do not want to look like, and why:
Where the mark must work: website header, social avatar, packaging, invoice, ad end-frame
Colour constraints (existing packaging, prints, or none):
Deliverables required: vector source, SVG, transparent PNG at 3 sizes,
  monochrome variant, stacked variant, colour and typeface list

Check on delivery

  • Vector source is included, not only flattened images. Without it, every future edit is a re-purchase.
  • The mark is legible at avatar size — shrink it to 48px and look at it on your phone.
  • Commercial use and any trademark filing intentions are stated in writing before you order, not assumed. Terms differ by seller and by package.
  • Typefaces are named, and where licensing is required for commercial use, that is disclosed.

Fuller brief, the file set to insist on, and the licensing questions to settle first: commissioning a logo and brand kit.

Where to look: logo design listings, or Fiverr's own Logo Maker if you want to customise a pre-made mark instead of commissioning one.

2. Product photo cleanup and lifestyle composites

Outsource when you have real photographs of the real product, even phone shots on a white sheet. Do it yourself when you do not yet have the product in hand — no retoucher can invent a texture that has never been photographed, and asking for it is how stores end up shipping something that does not match the listing.

This is the highest-leverage spend on the list, because the main image is doing the work of a salesperson on every ad, listing, and search result.

Full brief and file list: product photo brief template.

Where to look: image editing and retouching listings.

3. Product page copy

Outsource when you can hand over raw material: the spec sheet, the three objections you keep hearing, and any real customer messages. Do it yourself when you have none of that. A copywriter with no inputs will write adjectives.

What a usable deliverable contains: a title that names the product and its one differentiator, a short block that answers the objection stopping the purchase, scannable specifics rather than "premium quality", and an FAQ built from questions people have actually asked you.

Brief

Product, price, and what it replaces for the buyer:
Specs that matter (materials, sizes, compatibility, care):
The three objections I hear most, verbatim:
Two competitor pages I think are strong, and why:
Voice: plain / technical / playful — pick one, no mixing
Word budget per section, and where it appears in the theme
Deliverables: copy in a doc with section headers matching my theme blocks

What to gather before you hire a writer, and the claims you must never delegate: briefing product page copy.

Where to look: website and product copywriting listings.

4. Landing page or theme customisation

Outsource when the structure is decided and you need it built or restyled. Do it yourself when you are still guessing the offer — pay for a build after the offer stops changing weekly.

Scope this by naming sections in order, not by saying "make it look premium". A brief that reads "hero with one claim and one button, then three-icon proof row, then reviews, then FAQ, then sticky add-to-cart on mobile" gets built once. "Modern and clean" gets rebuilt three times.

Store access is the risk step. Anything requiring theme or admin access deserves care: create a staff account with the narrowest permissions that lets the work happen, ask the seller to work on a duplicated theme rather than the live one, and remove the access when the job closes. Never hand over your account password.

Section-by-section scoping, app and dependency questions, and how to hand over access: briefing a landing page or theme build.

Where to look: website development listings, or Shopify-specific sellers if your work is theme-level.

5. First paid-ad video creative

Outsource when you have footage or product stills and a claim you want tested. Do it yourself when you have not written the hook — the edit cannot rescue a hook nobody wrote.

Ad editing is a different craft from narrative editing. Vetting criteria and the brief: vetting a video editor for paid ads.

Where to look: video editing listings.

6. Welcome and abandoned-cart email flows

Outsource when your email platform is connected and sending. Do it yourself when it is not — copy for flows that cannot fire is inventory nobody uses.

Two flows are worth building before any others: the welcome sequence for people who signed up but have not bought, and the abandoned-cart sequence. Ask for subject lines, preview text, body copy, and the send delays, structured as a table you can drop into your platform. Ask explicitly whether the deliverable is copy only or copy plus platform setup; that boundary is where this order turns into a dispute. Step table, compliance, and the brief: briefing welcome and abandoned-cart flows.

Where to look: email marketing listings.

7. Pre-launch store QA

Outsource when the store is otherwise finished. Do it yourself when you are still adding products — QA on a moving target is wasted.

This is the cheapest insurance on the list and the first thing dropped when the launch date gets close. Ask for a written pass over: checkout on a real mobile device, tax and shipping rules per market, analytics and conversion tracking firing on the right events, broken links and orphan pages, mobile layout on a small viewport, page speed on the templates that matter, and the transactional emails a real order triggers.

Require the deliverable as a defect list with severity and steps to reproduce — not a screen recording with commentary. A list is actionable; a video is homework. Coverage list, severity tiers, and the test-order rules: commissioning pre-launch store QA.

What determines the quote you get

Listings on any freelance marketplace usually price in tiers. The differences between those tiers are consistent across categories, so you can read a quote quickly once you know what to look at:

Factors that move a freelance quote, in rough order of impact
FactorWhat to confirm before ordering
Scope countNumber of concepts, images, variants, or page sections included — and the price per extra unit.
Source filesWhether editable originals ship, or only flattened exports.
Usage rightsWhat you may do with the deliverable commercially. Ask in writing; it varies by seller and package.
RevisionsHow many rounds, what counts as a revision versus a new brief, and the cost after that.
TurnaroundStandard delivery time and the rush fee. Rush pricing is where budgets quietly double.
Third-party costsStock footage, fonts, music, or plugin licences — whose account they are bought on, and who keeps them.

Order of operations

  1. Product photos, because everything downstream reuses them.
  2. Product page copy, because the ad promise must match the page.
  3. Logo and brand kit, once the name is final.
  4. Landing page or theme work, once the offer stops moving.
  5. Ad creative, after the page it sends traffic to exists.
  6. Email flows, once sending works.
  7. QA, last, on a frozen store.
Nothing here requires you to be a designer, editor, or developer. It requires you to decide what you want, write it down, and check what arrives against what you asked for. That is the entire skill.
