Vetting a seller in any category

Editor, DTC Outsourcing Playbook

Updated on 10 Sep 2026 · 10 min

Vetting a seller in any category is not a talent judgement: you have never done the job, you cannot separate a competent edit from an excellent one, and you know it — so the decision falls back onto the rating and the order count, which are the two least specific things on the page. Vetting only becomes tractable once you stop trying to judge the craft and start testing the three things you can actually assess.

Key takeaways

  • You are not judging talent. Three questions carry almost all of the predictive weight available before you order.
  • Has this seller done this exact job before, did they understand the brief, and do the terms leave anything to argue about.
  • Keep the transaction on the platform. Off the platform, you are unprotected.

You are not judging talent

Three questions are answerable without any craft knowledge at all, and they carry almost all of the predictive weight available to you before you order.

Has this seller done this exact job before? Not this category — this job, for a buyer shaped roughly like you. Did they understand the brief? One message settles it, before any money moves. Do the terms leave anything to argue about? Deliverables, formats, source files, usage rights, revisions, and who buys third-party licences are all decidable in advance, in writing.

Everything else — taste, pace, whether they are pleasant to work with when a deadline slips — you learn by ordering. The three above you can learn first, and they describe the delivery better than a score does.

Vet in depth when the deliverable is load-bearing: the main product image, a theme build, anything that needs access to your store, anything whose failure moves your launch date. Order and find out when the job is small, self-contained, and easy to reorder elsewhere — a favicon, a single background removal, a caption file. Vetting effort should scale with what breaks if the delivery is wrong, not with how nervous you feel while browsing.

This page is the method; the briefs live elsewhere. What you order determines what you can check, so write the brief first: logo and brand kit, product page copy, paid-ads video, or the full launch checklist if you have not yet decided what to hand off.

What a portfolio proves, and what it does not

Samples in your category are not samples of your job. An illustrator with a rich editorial portfolio has not necessarily produced a mark that survives at avatar size. A retoucher full of glossy bottles has not necessarily kept the weave in a textile. The category is the shelf you found them on; what you are looking for is the artefact.

So read their work against your deliverable list, item by item. If the closest match is adjacent rather than the same, that does not disqualify anyone — it tells you your order will be their first attempt at it, and that the risk lands in your revision rounds and your calendar rather than in their confidence. Decide whether you have room for that before you order, not afterwards.

A portfolio is also a curated best-of, selected by the seller, sometimes from a job where the client's brief was better than yours. It says very little about the median delivery, which is the thing you are buying. Ask directly what a typical order in this package looks like, and what the buyer had to supply for it to go well. A seller who answers that plainly is describing a process. A seller who answers by pointing back at the showpiece has told you the showpiece was an exception.

Where a sample is obviously the output of a team, ask which parts were theirs. It is a fair question, the answer is quick, and a specific answer is worth more than the sample was.

Reading reviews like a professional

Look at the scores of the sellers currently on your screen. If they sit within a hair of one another, the number has already told you everything it can, and it was not much. The text is where the information is.

Read the critical reviews first, then read the seller's replies to them. The reply is the only place on the listing where you get to watch this person handle a problem. A reply that meets a fair complaint with what was done about it is exactly the behaviour you want when your own order goes sideways. A reply that argues with the buyer, questions their competence, or explains why the complaint does not count is a preview of your dispute, drafted in advance by the person you would be disputing with.

Then filter for relevance. A stack of praise for work unlike yours is weaker evidence than one detailed review describing your job. Read for repetition rather than sentiment: the same friction named by different buyers — deadlines slipping, extras appearing after work started, days of silence mid-job — is a property of how the seller operates, not an unlucky client.

Thin review history is not a red flag by itself. It is a reason to lean harder on the message test and the written terms, neither of which requires you to spend anything.

The pre-order message test

Send one question that cannot be answered from a saved reply. It has to require them to have read your brief, which means the brief must exist before you send it.

Useful questions are specific and slightly awkward. Given that the product is translucent and the background must stay pure white, how would you handle the edge? My hook line is fixed and the footage is all handheld — which of those two constraints would you push back on? Neither can be answered without engaging your material.

Judge the reply, not its speed. There are three outcomes. It engages your specifics and commits to an approach, which is good. It asks you a clarifying question before quoting anything, which is better. Or it arrives as a paragraph that would fit any buyer in the category, thanking you for reaching out and inviting you to place the order — in which case you have your answer, and it cost you one message.

The clarifying question deserves its own weight. Someone who asks what you meant before taking your money is showing the one trait that most reliably separates a clean delivery from three rounds of rework: they would rather resolve an ambiguity now than deliver something defensible and wrong. Nothing else visible on a listing predicts that.

Terms that make a dispute unnecessary

A dispute needs a disagreement about what was ordered. Remove that and a quality complaint becomes a revision request, which is a conversation rather than a fight. Every item below is decidable in writing before you pay, and each one is an argument you have chosen not to have.

  • Deliverables, enumerated and countable. "Full package" and "all files" are not deliverables. Name the items, the formats, and the quantity of each.
  • Source or editable files, stated as included or not. If they are not included, every later change returns to this seller as a new order.
  • Usage rights in plain words. Where you may use the work — store, ads, packaging, print, resale — and what is restricted. This varies by seller and by package, so ask rather than assume.
  • The revision boundary. Not the number of rounds but the definition: what counts as a revision, and what counts as a new brief. Unlimited revisions with no definition of a revision is not generosity, it is an unresolved argument sitting in your order.
  • When revisions happen. Whether they follow a first draft, or a review point you both agreed, or only the final delivery. This changes how much rework a single misread costs you.
  • Turnaround, and what a rush actually changes. Which parts of the scope get compressed, whether revision rounds shrink, and whether a surcharge applies — quoted before you order, not on the day.
  • Third-party assets. Whether the job needs stock footage, a font licence, music, or a paid plugin; who buys them; and whose account they are held on.
A licence bought on the seller's account is not your licence. Fonts, stock media, music, and paid plugins are the quiet way a finished job becomes unusable six months later, when the seller is gone and the licence went with them. Agree before ordering that anything licensed for your store is bought in your name, on your account, and that the receipt or licence reference arrives with the delivery. Check the asset provider's current terms yourself — what a licence permits for commercial and advertising use is theirs to change.

Scope creep runs both ways

The seller direction is familiar: the job was agreed as one thing, and extras appear once work has started. The other direction is your own brief, and it is the one buyers do not count.

You order a logo, then remember the social avatar, then the favicon, then something legible on an invoice. You order product copy, then decide the section headings should change to match a theme you are still editing. Each addition feels trivial to you because you can see the whole store. The seller can see the brief.

A brief that grows after the order produces a bad delivery honestly. The seller executes what they were given, you receive something that no longer matches what you now want, and nobody has behaved badly. The revision rounds get spent absorbing your new thinking rather than correcting their work, so the real defects ship.

The fix is not negotiation. It is writing the brief fully while changing your mind is still free — the deliverable list, the formats, the constraints, the things that must not change. That is what the rest of this site is for: the launch checklist decides which jobs deserve an order at all, and each category brief, such as the product page copy brief, forces the decisions that would otherwise surface mid-job as one small thing.

When you do want something added, name it as an addition rather than smuggling it in as a revision. Saying so costs you nothing and keeps you working with a seller who is not quietly absorbing scope, because the seller who absorbs scope is the seller who stops answering messages.

A small paid trial, or the whole job

Buy a deliberately small first order when the small version exercises the same skill as the large one: one image from a set of twelve, one product page out of eight, one email from a flow of five. You learn how they work, how they write, and whether they read a brief, on a job you can discard without losing anything but time.

Commission the whole job when the small version does not exercise the skill in question. One page of a five-page theme build tells you nothing about how the build behaves on a phone at checkout. A single logo concept tells you nothing about whether the mark holds as a monochrome variant on packaging, which is the check that matters once you are ordering labels. Splitting a job like that buys you a fragment and a second briefing.

A trial also has a cost buyers forget: two rounds of briefing, two message threads, two waits. Where the delivery is easy to check and easy to redo, a small first order is the most honest information available before you commit the whole thing. Where the job is structural, the trial delays the launch and answers a question you did not need answered.

Either way, the free checks come first. Each of these is verifiable from the listing or from a single message.

Keep the transaction on the platform

A seller who suggests moving the job to a private arrangement — a direct transfer, a different chat app, an invoice outside the order — may be entirely honest and simply tired of fees. It does not matter, because what you would be giving up is the record.

An order is not only a payment. It is a timestamped brief, a delivery, a revision history, and a message thread in which the two of you agreed what was included. If something later needs resolving, that thread is the argument. In a private arrangement the argument is your memory against theirs, with nobody obliged to look at either.

Off the platform, you are unprotected. Keep the brief, the confirmations, the deliverables, and every scope change inside the order thread — including the answers you got before ordering, which is the practical reason to ask them there rather than by email. Read the platform's current resolution and refund process before you need it rather than after, because those terms belong to the platform and no guide can tell you today's version.

Check on delivery

  • Count the deliverables against your own list, not against the seller's delivery note. Every item, in the format you named, at the quantity you ordered.
  • Confirm source or editable files arrived where you agreed them. Raise it while the order is still open; afterwards it becomes a fresh purchase.
  • Check usage rights match what was confirmed in the thread, including any stock media, font, music, or plugin licence, and whose account it was bought on.
  • Write every revision request against a line of the original brief. If you cannot point to the line, you are commissioning something new, and saying so keeps the seller cooperative.
  • Test the work where it will live — the phone, the theme, the ad placement, the inbox — before you close the order, not after you have built around it.
  • Leave the evidence in the order thread. Brief, answers, delivery, revisions, and any agreed change, all in one place where someone else could follow it.

Where to look: marketplace listings across every category. The checks above are category-agnostic; the craft-specific criteria sit in the sibling guides linked at the top of this page.

Vetting is not a talent assessment you were never qualified to make. It is three checkable questions asked before any money moves: has this exact job been done before, was the brief read, and do both of us agree what arrives. Get those three into writing and much of what gets called a bad freelancer experience simply does not happen to you.

FAQ

What can I assess before ordering a freelancer?

Three questions, with no craft knowledge required: has this seller done this exact job before, did they understand the brief, and do the terms leave anything to argue about.

What does a portfolio prove?

Samples in your category are not samples of your job. The category is the shelf you found them on; what you are looking for is the artefact.

Should I take a freelance job off the platform?

No. Off the platform, you are unprotected. Keep the brief, the confirmations, the deliverables, and every scope change inside the order thread.

When is a small paid trial better than the whole job?

When the deliverable is load-bearing and you have not seen this exact job from this seller. Order and find out when the job is small, self-contained, and easy to reorder elsewhere.
