About this site

Editor, DTC Outsourcing Playbook

Updated on 10 Sep 2026

Outsourcing a task is easy. Deciding what to outsource, describing it well enough that a stranger can execute it, and checking what arrives against what you asked for — that is where the money goes. This site is about the second part, because the first part is already well covered and the third part is where most of the loss happens.

Key takeaways

  • This site is about deciding what to outsource, writing a brief a stranger can execute, and checking what arrives.
  • No price tables for other people's work, no seller endorsements, no invented statistics.
  • Funding and conflicts are named on the disclosure page.

The problem these guides address

A first-time store owner has a long list and no idea which items reward paying someone. So they do the wrong half themselves, badly and slowly, then buy the other half from a freelancer using a brief that reads "make it look professional". What comes back is a competent answer to a question nobody asked, and the rework costs more than the original order.

Almost none of that is the freelancer's fault. A brief that transfers no decisions forces the seller to make those decisions for you, in the dark, on a deadline. The fix is unglamorous: decide first, write it down, then buy.

How the guides are written

  • Decision rule before purchase instruction. Every task starts with a rule for keeping it in-house. Some things should not be outsourced yet, and saying so is more useful than a brief template.
  • No price tables for other people's work. Freelance rates move constantly and vary by seller, scope, and turnaround. A number published today misleads someone reading in six months. The guides explain what pushes a quote up instead, so you can read a live listing yourself. My own prices are on the services page, because those are the one set of rates I can actually be held to.
  • No third-party seller endorsements. Nobody here has bought from every seller in a category, so naming favourites would be dressing up a small sample as a recommendation. The guides teach vetting criteria and let you apply them. Work sold on this site is my own, priced openly, and is not an endorsement of anyone else.
  • No invented statistics. If a claim would need data to support it, it is either sourced or not made.
  • Briefs you can paste. Every guide ends with something copyable, because a brief that stays theoretical never gets written.

What this site is not

It does not resell other people's services, act as an agency, or broker introductions. It does not review individual sellers, run rankings, or accept payment for placement in a guide. If a page ever recommends something because someone paid for the mention, this paragraph will be wrong and the site will have stopped being worth reading.

It does take on four engineering jobs on the services page. Those are my own work at a fixed price, not an agency wrapping someone else's. Direct bank transfer is the default; the first package is also available through Contra as an optional platform checkout. The guides still do not recommend me: the offer lives on its own page, and none of the briefs was written to make buying from me look like the answer. One of the four — a page-speed fix — overlaps with what two guides tell you to check, and that overlap is named on the disclosure page rather than left for you to find.

How it is funded

Honestly and in full on the disclosure page, including the current status. Two paths, only one of them open: paid engineering work, normally paid by direct bank transfer with Contra available for the first package, and an affiliate application that has not been approved. The short version of the second path: links in the guides point to marketplace category pages so you land on current listings, and that path has earned nothing yet.

Who writes it

One person, not a team or a media company. The site was published in September 2026 and is new — there is no long archive behind it and no audience to inherit trust from. The guides are written from the buyer's side of these orders: what had to be decided before ordering, what a thin brief produced, and what should have been checked before releasing payment. The engineering jobs are the same person on the other side of that table, which is why the conflict is named on the disclosure page rather than implied away.

That is the useful limit to state plainly. This is not a design, copywriting, or development tutorial, and it does not pretend to teach the crafts being bought. It teaches the part the buyer cannot delegate: deciding what you want, writing it down, and checking what arrives. The four jobs I sell are the ones where I can be held to that same standard.

Corrections

If something here is wrong, out of date, or reads as an endorsement it should not, say so. Contact details are on the disclosure page. Corrections get made on the page itself, not in a footnote.

Start where the launch actually blocks. The launch checklist decides what to buy at all; the vetting guide decides who to buy it from. Everything else on the guides page is a brief for one specific job.
