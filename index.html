Brief templates a freelancer can execute — and what to check on delivery

Most of what goes wrong with a first outsourced job is decided before anyone starts work: the brief left out the one thing that mattered, so the delivery is technically what you asked for and useless to you. Each guide here ends in a brief you can paste and a list to check against what comes back.

Or have the engineering built for you — from $220 Store data automation, a custom MCP server for Claude, or price and stock monitoring. Decide before you order

Three questions that are cheaper to settle before the brief than after the delivery.

  • What is worth paying for at all — the tasks worth buying before your first sale, each with a rule for doing it yourself instead.
  • Fixed price or hourly — one test decides which contract shape a job wants, and forcing an open scope into a fixed price is where disputes come from.
  • Reading a listing and vetting a seller — how to judge a seller in a field you do not work in, and the terms to agree in writing first.

How these guides are written

  • No price tables for other people's work. Freelance rates move constantly and vary by seller, scope, and turnaround. Instead of stale numbers, each guide explains what pushes a quote up so you can read a live listing yourself. My own prices live on the services page.
  • No third-party seller endorsements. Nobody here has bought from every seller in a category, so the guides teach vetting criteria rather than naming names. Work sold here is my own, priced openly, and is not an endorsement of anyone else.
  • No invented statistics. If a claim would need data behind it, it is either sourced or not made.
  • Briefs you can paste. Rework caused by a thin brief is what makes outsourcing expensive. Every guide ends with something copyable.
