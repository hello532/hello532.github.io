How these guides are written

  • No price tables for other people's work. Freelance rates move constantly and vary by seller, scope, and turnaround. Instead of stale numbers, each guide explains what pushes a quote up so you can read a live listing yourself. My own prices live on the services page.
  • No third-party seller endorsements. Nobody here has bought from every seller in a category, so the guides teach vetting criteria rather than naming names. Work sold here is my own, priced openly, and is not an endorsement of anyone else.
  • No invented statistics. If a claim would need data behind it, it is either sourced or not made.
  • Briefs you can paste. Rework caused by a thin brief is what makes outsourcing expensive. Every guide ends with something copyable.

FAQ

What is a DTC outsourcing brief a freelancer can execute?

A brief that transfers the decisions only you can make — the artefact, the constraints, and what must not change — so a stranger can produce something you can check on delivery. Each guide on this site ends in a pasteable brief and a list to check against what comes back.

What should I check when outsourced work comes back?

Count the deliverables against your own list, in the formats you named. Check the work where it will live — the phone, the theme, the inbox — before you release payment. A delivery that is technically what you asked for can still be useless if the brief left out the one thing that mattered.

Is email flow work a DTC brand CX outsourcing job?

Yes. Welcome and abandoned-cart flows are CX work: they talk to people who already did something, and they fail in setup and exits rather than in subject lines. Brief them as a job with a step table, not as a design mood.

Should a launch job be fixed price or hourly?

One test: can you enumerate the deliverable before work starts. If you can write a countable list of artefacts, buy a fixed price and spend your effort on the brief. If you cannot, close the scope first or pay for discovery by the hour with a cap.
